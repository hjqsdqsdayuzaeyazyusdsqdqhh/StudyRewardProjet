About Alzheimer Disease

Alzheimer disease is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder and the most common cause of dementia, affecting memory and cognitive function. Clinical trials explore anti-amyloid antibodies, tau-targeting therapies, neuroprotective agents, and lifestyle interventions.

Common Symptoms

Memory loss especially of recent events, difficulty planning or solving problems, confusion with time or place, trouble understanding visual images, problems with speaking or writing, misplacing items, decreased judgment, withdrawal from social activities

Risk Factors

Advancing age, family history, genetics including APOE-e4, cardiovascular risk factors, head injury, poor sleep, sedentary lifestyle, limited cognitive engagement

Treatment Overview

Current treatments provide symptomatic relief but do not stop disease progression. Clinical trials study disease-modifying therapies including monoclonal antibodies targeting amyloid and tau, and combination approaches.

Why Participate?

Alzheimer disease trials offer access to potentially disease-modifying therapies that may slow cognitive decline.

Frequently Asked Questions About Alzheimer Disease Clinical Trials

Alzheimer Disease clinical trials are research studies that test new treatments, medications, devices, or interventions for managing or treating alzheimer disease. They help determine the safety and effectiveness of potential new approaches.
Eligibility varies by study. Factors include the type and severity of alzheimer disease, age, overall health status, and prior treatment history. Each study has specific inclusion and exclusion criteria.
Compensation depends on the study duration, number of visits, and procedures involved. Participants may receive payments ranging from $50 to $300 per visit, with total compensation varying by study.
All clinical trials follow strict safety protocols and are reviewed by Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) to protect participants. Potential risks are explained during the informed consent process before enrollment.
