Statistical Concepts in Clinical Trials Made Simple

Plain-English explanations of key statistical concepts used in clinical trials, from p-values to confidence intervals.

By Dr. Michael Chen April 28, 2026

Plain-English explanations of key statistical concepts used in clinical trials, from p-values to confidence intervals.

Why Statistics Matter in Clinical Research

Statistics help researchers determine whether treatment effects are real or simply due to chance. Without statistics, it would be impossible to know whether a new treatment truly works or if observed improvements happened randomly. Statistical analysis allows researchers to draw reliable conclusions from data collected from a sample of participants and apply those conclusions to the broader population. Understanding basic statistics helps you evaluate study results critically.

P-Values and Statistical Significance

A p-value measures the probability that observed results occurred by chance alone. A p-value of 0.05 or less is typically considered statistically significant, meaning there is less than a 5 percent probability that the results are due to chance. Lower p-values indicate stronger evidence against chance. However, statistical significance does not necessarily mean clinical significance, which considers whether the effect is large enough to matter in practice.

Confidence Intervals Explained

A confidence interval provides a range of values that likely contains the true treatment effect. A 95 percent confidence interval means that if the study were repeated many times, 95 percent of the intervals would contain the true effect. Narrow confidence intervals indicate more precise estimates, while wide intervals suggest more uncertainty. Confidence intervals are often more informative than p-values because they show the range of possible effects.

Understanding Effect Size and Number Needed to Treat

Effect size measures the magnitude of a treatment's effect, not just whether it is statistically significant. Number needed to treat or NNT indicates how many people need to receive the treatment for one person to benefit. A lower NNT means a more effective treatment. For example, an NNT of 10 means 10 people must be treated for one person to experience the beneficial outcome. These measures help translate statistical results into clinical practice.

Key Takeaways

  • Statistics determine whether treatment effects are real or due to chance, enabling reliable conclusions from research data.
  • P-values under 0.05 indicate statistical significance, but significant results may not always be clinically meaningful.
  • Confidence intervals show the range of likely treatment effects; narrower intervals indicate more precise estimates.
  • Effect size and number needed to treat help translate statistical findings into practical clinical decisions.
