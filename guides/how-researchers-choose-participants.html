How Researchers Choose Clinical Trial Participants

Behind the scenes look at how clinical trial participants are selected, including screening, randomization, and recruitment strategies.

By Dr. Michael Chen May 7, 2026

Behind the scenes look at how clinical trial participants are selected, including screening, randomization, and recruitment strategies.

The Screening and Selection Process

Clinical trial screening is a multi-step process designed to identify participants who meet the study's eligibility criteria. Initial pre-screening often happens by phone or online questionnaire to quickly assess basic requirements. The formal screening visit includes medical history review, physical examination, and laboratory tests. Researchers verify that you meet all inclusion criteria and have no exclusion criteria that would make participation unsafe. This careful selection ensures both participant safety and reliable study results.

Recruitment Strategies Research Teams Use

Research teams use various strategies to find potential participants. Common approaches include patient databases, physician referrals, support group outreach, social media advertising, and community partnerships. Some studies use electronic health records to identify potential participants who have given permission to be contacted. ClinicalTrials.gov and StudyReward are also important recruitment tools that connect researchers with interested volunteers.

Randomization: How Groups Are Assigned

In randomized trials, participants are assigned to treatment groups by chance, much like flipping a coin. This process ensures that groups are comparable and reduces bias in the results. Randomization is typically done by computer using specialized software. Neither you nor the researcher can choose which group you join. Some studies use stratified randomization to ensure balanced distribution of key characteristics like age or disease severity across groups.

Why Some Applicants Are Not Selected

Many factors can prevent someone from being selected for a clinical trial. Common reasons include not meeting specific eligibility criteria, laboratory values outside acceptable ranges, medical history that increases risk, or the study reaching its enrollment target. Being declined for one study does not reflect on your health or suitability for other research. Each trial has unique criteria, and you may qualify for many other studies.

Key Takeaways

  • Screening involves pre-screening, medical history review, physical exam, and lab tests to verify eligibility.
  • Research teams use patient databases, physician referrals, advertising, and community outreach to recruit participants.
  • Randomization assigns participants to treatment groups by computer, preventing bias in group assignment.
  • Being declined for one trial is common and does not indicate anything about your health or eligibility for other studies.
